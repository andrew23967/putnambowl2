PutnamBowl Analysis

Can a simple strategy
beat PutnamBowl?

Underdogs pay more and win less. The scoring is built so the two even out, which means any edge has to come from real NFL results disagreeing with the odds. Three rules are tested here against every season since 2016: pick underdogs at a fixed rate, pick underdogs above a point threshold, and always pick certain teams.

Every chart shows the same two things: where the strategy actually landed, and a shaded band it has to escape to mean anything. The band is wider than a textbook confidence interval on purpose. Test enough rules and one of them looks good by luck, so the threshold is raised to account for every rule tested at once — a Bonferroni correction. Inside the band is noise. Outside it is a finding.

Strategy 1

Pick underdogs X% of the time

For every game, flip a biased coin: underdog with probability X, favourite otherwise. No matchups, no lines, no judgement — just a fixed preference, swept from 0% to 100%.

Each rate is played out over every season {{ config.n_trials }} times. The line is the average season score it produced; the band is the range that counts as no different from always picking favourites.

Strategy 2

Pick underdogs only when the payout is large enough

Take the underdog only when the payout justifies it. Small underdogs may not be worth the risk; large ones might be. Games are grouped by the underdog's point value to find out where, if anywhere, the line tips.

Each bar is how much a bucket gained or lost per game by taking the underdog instead of the favourite. Green favours the underdog, red the favourite. The band widens where a bucket holds fewer games, because thin evidence has to be stronger to count.

Strategy 3

Always pick certain teams

Ignore the odds and always back the same teams. Some franchises are reliably good — perhaps backing them, or fading the reliably bad ones, beat the line often enough to pay.

Verdict

No simple strategy beats PutnamBowl

All three rules land in the same place: none of them beats picking at random once the noise is accounted for. The odds-based scoring balances risk against reward well enough that a blunt preference — for underdogs, for big payouts, for particular teams — buys you nothing.

These rules are deliberately blunt. They know nothing about form, injuries, weather, home field, or what week it is. A better strategy is surely out there; it just won't fit on one line.

Finding it is the point. The data is here — go beat it.
